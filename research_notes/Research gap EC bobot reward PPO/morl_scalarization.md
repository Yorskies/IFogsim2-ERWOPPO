# Multi-objective RL: linear scalarization, weight selection, Pareto policy sets, and when weights change the policy

Context: study = evolutionary search over 5 linear reward weights (latency, energy, deadline violation, load imbalance, failure) for PPO offloading, scored by a fixed aggregate (RMS of normalized costs). Finding: weights barely matter after enough training; 5 costs act like about 2 effective objectives.

Verification legend: items marked **[verified]** were confirmed from a publisher, proceedings or arXiv page during this research. **[recalled, verify]** means the bibliographic record was confirmed but the specific internal detail comes from prior knowledge of the paper and was not re-read in full text here.

## Q1. What do MORL surveys say about choosing weights a priori vs learning Pareto sets? When is a single weighted policy appropriate?

### Takeaway
Both main surveys (Roijers et al. 2013 JAIR; Hayes et al. 2022 JAAMAS) frame the choice through a *utility-based* lens. A single scalarized policy is appropriate only when the user's utility function is **known** before learning (the "known utility / known weights" scenario). If it is unknown, uncertain, dynamic, or needs decision support, the method should return a coverage set: a CCS when utility is linear, a Pareto front otherwise. Hayes et al. call a priori fixed weighting a "semi-blind manual process". The study sits in an unusual spot. Its evaluation utility (RMS of normalized costs) is fixed and known, but it is *non-linear*, while the training reward is linear. Under the surveys' logic, the question becomes whether some linear weight vector's optimal policy also maximizes that non-linear utility.

### Cited Findings
- **Roijers, Vamplew, Whiteson & Dazeley (2013)**, "A Survey of Multi-Objective Sequential Decision-Making", *JAIR* 48:67–113, DOI 10.1613/jair.3987 [verified]. It names three scenarios where turning the problem into a single objective is impossible, infeasible or undesirable. It classifies methods by scenario, scalarization function (linear vs. monotonically increasing) and policy type (deterministic or stochastic). The optimal solution is a single policy, a convex hull/convex coverage set (CCS) or a Pareto front. Computing the full Pareto front is often unnecessary, and a CCS of deterministic stationary policies is often enough. It suggests optimizing the expectation of the scalarized return when a policy is executed only once. — [JAIR](https://jair.org/index.php/jair/article/view/10836); [arXiv 1402.0590](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1402.0590)
- **Hayes, Rădulescu, Bargiacchi, Källström, Macfarlane, Reymond, Verstraeten, Zintgraf, Dazeley, Heintz, Howley, Irissappane, Mannion, Nowé, Ramos, Restelli, Vamplew & Roijers (2022)**, "A practical guide to multi-objective reinforcement learning and planning", *Autonomous Agents and Multi-Agent Systems (JAAMAS)* 36, article 26, DOI 10.1007/s10458-022-09552-y [verified]. A short version, "A Brief Guide to Multi-Objective RL and Planning", appeared as a JAAMAS-track paper at AAMAS 2023. — [arXiv 2103.09568](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.09568); [Springer PDF](https://rd.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s10458-022-09552-y.pdf); [AAMAS 2023 brief](https://www.southampton.ac.uk/~eg/AAMAS2023/pdfs/p1988.pdf)
  - Sec. 1: a priori scalarization is "a semi-blind manual process". Because only a subset of possible scalarizations is tried, the result may be "arbitrarily far away from optimal utility". With hand-set weights "we are only guessing as to the effects this might have on the policy". "The relationship between the reward weights and the actual objective outcomes may well be non-linear". Also, "not all human preferences can be handled by scalar additive reward functions". — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.09568)
  - Sec. 4 contrasts two approaches. In the **axiomatic approach**, "the optimal solution set ... is assumed to be the Pareto front". The **utility-based approach** aims to "derive the optimal solution set from the available knowledge about the utility function of the user". — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.09568)
  - Sec. 5.1 gives the scenario taxonomy: (a) unknown utility, (b) decision support, (c) known utility, (d) interactive decision support, (e) dynamic utility, (f) review and adjust. For known utility, "it is possible to learn a single policy that optimises the user's preferences", and Sec. 5.2.1 adds that "returning multiple policies is not necessary". — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.09568)
  - Sec. 5.2.2: "A linear utility function cannot accurately represent non-linear preferences." — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.09568)
  - Sec. 5.3 compares SER (scalarized expected return) and ESR (expected scalarized return). They give the same optimal policies for linear utility and can differ a lot for non-linear utility. — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.09568)
  - Sec. 3.2: the convex hull is "the set of policies that maximise the weighted sum over objectives for some weight vector". The CCS is a subset of it that contains a maximizing policy for every weight. With stochastic policies, "a CCS is sufficient to construct a CS for all possible (non-linear) monotonically increasing utility functions" under SER, via policy mixtures. — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.09568)
  - Sec. 7.2.2: naive search over the parameter (weight) space "may re-learn the same policy". Sec. 9.3: sampling weights uniformly from the simplex (NES baseline) "does not result in a spread-out coverage set". — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.09568)

### Inferences
- The study's setup (outer-loop EA tunes linear reward weights; candidates are judged by a fixed non-linear RMS utility) is a known-utility, single-policy scenario with a mismatch between training scalarization (linear) and evaluation utility (non-linear). In the surveys' terms, the EA is a black-box search over the CCS for the point that best serves the RMS utility. Hayes et al. Sec. 1 explicitly warns that this kind of trial-and-error scalarization tuning can be inefficient.
- An alternative the surveys point to is optimizing the known non-linear utility directly (ESR/SER with a non-linear scalarization). That would remove the weight-search layer altogether.
- Hayes Sec. 7.2.2 ("may re-learn the same policy") is a direct precedent for the study's finding that weights barely change the learned policy.

### Gaps
- Neither survey gives a quantitative rule for when the single-policy approach is "good enough". That has to be argued from CCS size (see Q2).

## Q2. When does changing linear weights change the optimal policy? Objective redundancy and correlated objectives

### Takeaway
The weighted-sum optimal policy changes only when the weight vector crosses into a different region of the CCS's piecewise-linear "scalarized value function". Each CCS policy is optimal over a polytope of weight space, bounded by "corner weights" (Roijers et al., OLS). If objectives are non-conflicting or strongly correlated, the CCS collapses to one or a few policies, so large regions of weight space yield the same policy. In many-objective optimization, a mature literature formalizes the same idea as "objective reduction": redundant objectives can be removed without changing the Pareto front or dominance structure (Brockhoff & Zitzler; Deb & Saxena; Saxena et al.). Linear scalarization also cannot reach concave (non-convex) parts of a Pareto front (Das & Dennis 1997 for MOO; Vamplew et al. 2008 for MORL).

### Cited Findings
- **Vamplew, Yearwood, Dazeley & Berry (2008)**, "On the Limitations of Scalarisation for Multi-objective Reinforcement Learning of Pareto Fronts", *AI 2008: Advances in Artificial Intelligence*, LNCS 5360, pp. 372–378, Springer [verified bibliographic record; full text not read]. It argues that MORL should approximate the Pareto front and that linear scalarization has fundamental shortcomings for finding Pareto-optimal policies, namely that it is restricted to convex regions of the front. — [Federation Univ. repository](https://researchonline.federation.edu.au/vital/access/manager/Repository/vital:3742); [researchr](https://researchr.org/publication/VamplewYDB08)
- Hayes et al. (2022) Sec. 8.1.1: when utility is linear, hypervolume is "not applicable" because policies in "all concave regions in the Pareto front" are "known to not improve utility". In other words, linear-utility optimizers never select concave-region policies. — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.09568)
- **Das & Dennis (1997)**, "A closer look at drawbacks of minimizing weighted sums of objectives for Pareto set generation in multicriteria optimization problems", *Structural Optimization* 14:63–69 [recalled, verify]. This is the classical MOO result that (i) weighted sums cannot find points on non-convex parts of the front, and (ii) an even spread of weights does not give an even spread of Pareto points. — no URL verified in this session
- **Roijers, Whiteson & Oliehoek (2015)**, "Computing Convex Coverage Sets for Faster Multi-objective Coordination", *JAIR* 52:399–443, DOI 10.1613/jair.4550 [verified]. CCS-based methods (CMOVE, VELS) and the Optimistic Linear Support (OLS) outer loop solve a finite series of scalarized instances at chosen weights to build the CCS. The number of distinct scalarized optima equals the CCS size. — [Oxford page](https://cs.ox.ac.uk/publications/publication9777-abstract.html); [Liverpool repository](https://livrepository.liverpool.ac.uk/id/eprint/2032561)
  - [recalled, verify] OLS (Roijers' thesis; Roijers et al. 2015) shows that the scalarized value V*(w) = max over π in the CCS of w·V^π is piecewise-linear and convex in w. New CCS members can be found only at "corner weights" where linear pieces meet. Within one piece, every weight gives the same optimal policy.
- **Brockhoff & Zitzler (2006)**, "Are All Objectives Necessary? On Dimensionality Reduction in Evolutionary Multiobjective Optimization", *PPSN IX*, LNCS 4193 [verified]. It introduces a formal framework and algorithms for removing objectives while preserving dominance relations. On four test problems, substantial reductions were possible. — [Springer Professional](https://www.springerprofessional.de/en/are-all-objectives-necessary-on-dimensionality-reduction-in-evol/1241462)
- **Brockhoff & Zitzler (2009)**, "Objective Reduction in Evolutionary Multiobjective Optimization: Theory and Applications", *Evolutionary Computation* 17(2):135–166, MIT Press [verified]. It defines conflict between objective sets and gives exact and greedy algorithms for the minimum objective subset (MOSS) and k-EMOSS (an objective subset of size k with minimal error). It is applied to radar waveform design and test functions. — [ETH Research Collection](https://www.research-collection.ethz.ch/handle/20.500.11850/567963)
- **Deb & Saxena (2006)**, "Searching for Pareto-optimal solutions through dimensionality reduction for certain large-dimensional multi-objective optimization problems", *IEEE CEC 2006* [recalled, verify title/venue]. It introduced PCA-NSGA-II, which combines principal component analysis on the objective-value correlation matrix with NSGA-II to find redundant (correlated) objectives. — summarized in [Saxena et al. 2013 (IAS repository)](https://repository.ias.ac.in/82728)
- **Saxena & Deb (2007)**, "Non-linear dimensionality reduction procedures for certain large-dimensional multi-objective optimization problems: employing correntropy and a novel maximum variance unfolding", *EMO 2007*, LNCS 4403 [verified chapter record]. Its C-PCA-NSGA-II and MVU-PCA-NSGA-II were tested up to 50 objectives. — [Springer Professional](https://www.springerprofessional.de/en/non-linear-dimensionality-reduction-procedures-for-certain-large/2766562)
- **Saxena, Duro, Tiwari, Deb & Zhang (2013)**, "Objective Reduction in Many-Objective Optimization: Linear and Nonlinear Algorithms", *IEEE Transactions on Evolutionary Computation* 17(1):77–99 [verified record]. It proposes L-PCA (linear) and NL-MVU-PCA (nonlinear) objective reduction and assumes "the existence of redundant objectives". It is the standard reference for correlation-based objective reduction. — [IAS repository](https://repository.ias.ac.in/82728); [CityU record](https://scholars.cityu.edu.hk/en/publications/objective-reduction-in-many-objective-optimization-linear-and-non/)
- Later work restates the definition. Redundant objectives can be dropped "without changing the structure of [the] original PF" (attributed to Gal & Hanne), and a non-conflicting pair makes one objective redundant. There is also an online (in-run) reduction variant (Soft Computing 2015, DOI 10.1007/s00500-015-1648-y) [verified record; authors not checked]. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00500-015-1648-y)

### Inferences
- The study's empirical result (the 5 costs behave like about 2 effective objectives, and weights barely matter after enough training) matches the textbook conditions under which linear weights are inert. (a) Correlated objectives: latency, deadline violation and failure plausibly move together. (b) A small CCS: if the CCS has only a handful of policies, most of the 4-D weight simplex maps to the same one or two policies. (c) Training noise: PPO seed variance can exceed the value differences between neighboring CCS policies, so weight effects disappear in the noise. The study can report this in the established language of "objective redundancy / objective reduction" (Brockhoff & Zitzler 2009; Saxena et al. 2013).
- Suggested diagnostic, derived from that literature: compute the correlation matrix or PCA of the 5 per-episode cost vectors across evaluated policies (the PCA-NSGA-II style). Also check the δ-error of dropping objectives (Brockhoff & Zitzler). Either would support the "about 2 effective objectives" claim quantitatively.
- A negative result like "weights do not matter" is consistent with theory only if conflict is low. If the costs truly conflicted, a fixed-RMS evaluator would still be sensitive to weights unless its optimum lies in a concave region that linear weights cannot reach. In that case all weight vectors would land on the same convex-hull vertex near it, which can also produce apparent weight insensitivity.

### Gaps
- I found no MORL (as opposed to EMO) paper that formally analyzes objective redundancy or correlated reward components in deep RL. This appears to be a genuine gap the study can claim, though the search was not exhaustive.
- The Das & Dennis (1997) and Deb & Saxena (2006) details were not re-verified online in this session.

## Q3. MORL with preference vectors or Pareto policy sets applied to task offloading / edge computing

### Takeaway
A small but growing body of work treats MEC offloading as an MOMDP. These include PPO-based preference-aware MORL (Yang et al., WiOpt 2023), a generalizable Discrete-SAC follow-up (Yang et al., arXiv 2025), MORL for dependent-task offloading with vector rewards (Song et al., FGCS 2022), and evolutionary multi-objective DRL that adjusts weights, PGMORL-like (Sun et al., arXiv 2025, UAV-MEC). Almost all use 2–3 objectives (delay, energy, sometimes cost) and evaluate with hypervolume. None that I found uses five objectives or studies weight insensitivity.

### Cited Findings
- **Yang, Wen, Zhang & Tang (2023)**, "Multi-objective Deep Reinforcement Learning for Mobile Edge Computing", arXiv 2307.14346; the arXiv comment says "Received by IEEE WiOpt 2023" [verified as arXiv; proceedings record not checked]. PPO-based MORL for multi-edge MEC that minimizes long-term energy and transmission delay. Preferences are treated as unknown or hard to specify in advance, and the paper reports handling preferences not seen in training. Hypervolume improves by up to 233.1% over benchmarks. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.14346)
- **Yang, Wen, Zhang & Tang (2025)**, "Generalizable Pareto-Optimal Offloading with Reinforcement Learning in Mobile Edge Computing" (GMORL), arXiv 2509.10474 [verified as arXiv; venue unverified]. Discrete Soft Actor-Critic for unknown preferences across multiple edge servers, energy and delay objectives, reported to beat benchmarks by up to 121.0%. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.10474v1)
- **Song, Xing, Wang, Luo, Dai & Li (2022)**, "Offloading dependent tasks in multi-access edge computing: A multi-objective reinforcement learning approach", *Future Generation Computer Systems* 128:333–348, DOI 10.1016/j.future.2021.10.013 [verified record; full text not read]. Objectives are completion time, device energy and edge usage charge. The paper explicitly criticizes earlier RL that "combine[s] these goals into a single weighted score". It uses an MOMDP with vector rewards and an improved MORL with tournament selection to retain previously learned policies. — [X-MOL](https://www.x-mol.com/paper/1451640096349388800); [Scinapse](https://www.scinapse.io/papers/3209477841)
- **Sun, Ma, Li, Sun, Wang, Niyato & Mao (2025)**, "Task Delay and Energy Consumption Minimization for Low-altitude MEC via Evolutionary Multi-objective Deep Reinforcement Learning", arXiv 2501.06410 [verified as arXiv preprint; no journal reference listed]. An MOMDP with delay and UAV energy, solved by multi-objective DRL "within an evolutionary framework" that adjusts weights to get non-dominated policies, plus target distribution learning (TDL). The abstract does not state whether the base learner is PPO or PGMORL. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.06410)
- A Brunel University work proposes a preference-based attention decoder for multi-objective energy/latency offloading using neural combinatorial optimization plus RL [verified that the PDF exists; authors, venue and year unverified]. — [Brunel BURA](https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/bitstream/2438/30986/3/FullText.pdf)

### Inferences
- The closest precedent to the study's design (an evolutionary outer loop over weights plus a DRL inner loop) is Sun et al. 2025 and PGMORL itself. Neither reports the "weights do not matter" phenomenon, and neither uses 5 objectives. That makes the study's negative or redundancy finding a novel contribution relative to the MEC-MORL literature.
- Most MEC-MORL papers report hypervolume over 2 objectives, where conflict (delay vs. energy) is strong by construction. Adding correlated costs such as deadline violation and failure is exactly the case that the objective-reduction literature predicts will collapse.

### Gaps
- IEEE IoT Journal and IEEE TMC MORL-offloading papers were not surfaced by these searches. A targeted IEEE Xplore search is needed; I did not find verifiable ones, so none are listed.
- The WiOpt 2023 proceedings entry for Yang et al. was not confirmed.

## Q4. How PGMORL works and how it is evaluated; evolutionary MORL follow-ups

### Takeaway
PGMORL (Xu et al., ICML 2020) is the canonical "evolutionary + linear-scalarized deep RL" method. It keeps a population of policies, and each generation selects (policy, weight) pairs for further PPO training on the weighted-sum reward. A per-policy analytical model predicts how the objectives will improve under each candidate weight, and the pairs are chosen to maximize the predicted hypervolume and sparsity of the resulting front. It is evaluated by hypervolume and sparsity on 7 MuJoCo multi-objective tasks. Follow-ups include the MORL/D decomposition framework (Felten et al., JAIR 2024), C-MORL (Liu et al., ICLR 2025), PCN (Reymond et al., AAMAS 2022) and the envelope Q-learning line (Yang et al., NeurIPS 2019).

### Cited Findings
- **Xu, Tian, Ma, Rus, Sueda & Matusik (2020)**, "Prediction-Guided Multi-Objective Reinforcement Learning for Continuous Robot Control", *ICML 2020*, PMLR 119:10607–10616 [verified]. An evolutionary algorithm built on a state-of-the-art RL algorithm. In each generation an analytical model per policy predicts the expected improvement along each objective, and the predictions select which policy-weight pairs to train next. A continuous Pareto set is built by Pareto analysis and interpolation. Seven continuous-action MORL environments form a new benchmark. It finds "a much denser and higher-quality set of Pareto policies" than existing algorithms. Code: github.com/mit-gfx/PGMORL. — [PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v119/xu20h.html); [project page](https://people.csail.mit.edu/jiex/papers/PGMORL/)
  - [recalled, verify in paper] Internal details: (1) a warm-up stage trains several policies with evenly spread weights using PPO, with weighted-sum scalarization of the vector advantage; (2) a hyperbolic improvement model is fitted per policy from past (weight, objective-change) data; (3) greedy task selection maximizes the mixed hypervolume and sparsity of the predicted front; (4) the Pareto archive is kept in performance buffers. Baselines were Radial Algorithm (RA), Pareto Following (PFA), an MOEA baseline and meta-learning MORL (META). Environments were MO-Walker2d, HalfCheetah, Hopper, Ant, Swimmer and Humanoid (2-objective) plus Hopper with 3 objectives.
  - Hayes et al. (2022) Sec. 8.1.2 credits Xu et al. with proposing the **sparsity** metric (lower is better). — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.09568)
- **Yang, Sun & Narasimhan (2019)**, "A Generalized Algorithm for Multi-Objective Reinforcement Learning and Policy Adaptation", *NeurIPS 2019* [verified]. A generalized (envelope) Bellman equation learns one preference-conditioned network covering optimal policies for all linear preferences. It infers unknown preferences from few samples and is evaluated on Deep Sea Treasure, Fruit Tree Navigation, task-oriented dialog and multi-objective SuperMario. It also notes the optimal policy "can shift considerably" with preference. — [NeurIPS](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2019/hash/4a46fbfca3f1465a27b210f4bdfe6ab3-Abstract.html); [arXiv 1908.08342](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.08342v2)
- **Reymond, Bargiacchi & Nowé (2022)**, "Pareto Conditioned Networks", *AAMAS 2022*, pp. 1110–1118 [verified]. A single network conditioned on the desired return represents all non-dominated policies. It is trained in a supervised way to avoid moving-target instability, makes minimal assumptions about front shape and so handles non-convex fronts. — [arXiv 2204.05036](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.05036); [VUB](https://researchportal.vub.be/en/publications/pareto-conditioned-networks/)
- **Felten, Alegre, Nowé, Bazzan, Talbi, Danoy & da Silva (2023)**, "A Toolkit for Reliable Benchmarking and Research in Multi-Objective Reinforcement Learning", *NeurIPS 2023 Datasets & Benchmarks* [verified]. It introduces MO-Gymnasium (20+ environments, standard API), MORL-Baselines (PyTorch implementations of SOTA algorithms, PGMORL among them) and benchmark results on openrlbenchmark/W&B. — [NeurIPS](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/hash/4aa8891583f07ae200ba07843954caeb-Abstract.html); [MO-Gymnasium GitHub](https://github.com/Farama-Foundation/MO-Gymnasium/)
- **Felten, Talbi & Danoy (2024)**, "Multi-Objective Reinforcement Learning Based on Decomposition: A Taxonomy and Framework", *JAIR* 79:679–723, DOI 10.1613/jair.1.15702 [verified]. MORL/D connects MORL to MOEA/D-style decomposition and gives a taxonomy that covers PGMORL-like evolutionary weight-selection schemes. Its instantiations are comparable to SOTA on the benchmarks tested. — [JAIR](https://jair.org/index.php/jair/article/view/15702); [arXiv 2311.12495](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.12495)
- **Liu, Pan, et al. (2025)**, "Efficient Discovery of Pareto Front for Multi-Objective Reinforcement Learning" (C-MORL), *ICLR 2025* [verified]. Stage 1 trains preference-specific policies in parallel. Stage 2 is Pareto extension, using crowding-distance selection and constrained policy updates (interior-point relaxation) to fill gaps. It reports up to 35% larger hypervolume and 9% higher expected utility than SOTA, with up to 9 objectives. — [arXiv 2410.02236](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02236); [ICLR](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2025/poster/28888)
- Hayes et al. (2022) Sec. 9.3 compares MONES (multi-objective natural evolution strategies, a Pareto-set-learning ES) with NES over uniformly sampled weights. Sampled weights give a poorly spread coverage set. — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.09568)

### Inferences
- PGMORL's selection target (hypervolume plus sparsity of the front) differs from the study's target, which is a single fixed RMS utility. PGMORL searches weights to spread out a front. The study searches weights to optimize one non-linear scalar. That is closer to hyperparameter or "reward-weight tuning" than to Pareto-set MORL, and the paper should frame it that way.
- MORL-Baselines (which includes PGMORL, envelope Q-learning, PCN, GPI-LS, CAPQL and MORL/D) offers ready baselines if the study wants to place its EA against a standard Pareto-set method.

### Gaps
- PGMORL's internals were not re-read from full text in this session (PDF extraction failed). The details marked [recalled, verify] should be checked against PMLR v119 before citing.
- I did not find a paper that directly benchmarks several evolutionary MORL variants (PGMORL vs. ES-based vs. MOEA/D-style) head to head, apart from the MORL-Baselines/MORL-D comparisons.

## Q5. Evaluation metrics (hypervolume, expected utility, IGD) and how MORL papers compare methods

### Takeaway
Hypervolume (plus sparsity, after PGMORL) is the de facto MORL metric. The Hayes guide argues that hypervolume "do[es] not map to any real-world notion of value or utility". It recommends utility-based metrics: the Expected Utility Metric (EUM) over a prior on utility functions, and Maximum Utility Loss (MUL). The epsilon indicator is offered as an alternative that can show dominance between sets. IGD is common in EMO but rarely used in MORL, because it needs a reference front. For the study, the fixed RMS utility is effectively a degenerate EUM with a point-mass prior, which the guide treats as legitimate when the utility is known.

### Cited Findings
- Hayes et al. (2022) Sec. 8.1.1 on hypervolume: values "do not map to any real-world notion of value or utility". It is meaningful only when every non-dominated policy can contribute to utility, and it is not applicable under linear utility. — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.09568)
- Sec. 8.1.2: sparsity (Xu et al.), where lower is better. Sec. 8.1.3: the additive or multiplicative ε-indicator, which "can give an indication of whether one is better than the other". Sec. 8.1.4: the coverage ratio is criticized for equal precision/recall weighting and ε-dependence. IGD is not discussed. — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.09568)
- Sec. 8.2: EUM averages the best achievable utility in the set over a prior on utility functions, and "requires a good prior over possible scalarisation functions". MUL is worst-case utility loss. If the user's utility can be assessed at deployment, compare sets directly on it. Sec. 8.3: run a separate thorough policy evaluation before selection, rather than trusting training-time estimates. — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.09568)
- Sec. 9.4 (water management case): EUM nearly doubled at iteration 7 while hypervolume rose only about 30%, showing that hypervolume can misrepresent user utility. — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.09568)
- C-MORL (ICLR 2025) reports both hypervolume and expected utility, which reflects current practice. — [arXiv 2410.02236](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02236)
- MEC-MORL papers (Yang et al. 2023) report hypervolume only. — [arXiv 2307.14346](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.14346)
- The MORL-Baselines/MO-Gymnasium toolkit standardizes evaluation across algorithms; the paper itself lists the metrics (hypervolume, EUM, sparsity, cardinality), [recalled, verify]. — [NeurIPS 2023](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/hash/4aa8891583f07ae200ba07843954caeb-Abstract.html)

### Inferences
- The study's single fixed RMS evaluator is defensible under Hayes et al. Sec. 8.2 (known utility means evaluating directly on that utility). But it hides weight effects that would show up on a front, because two policies with different trade-offs can have similar RMS. If the study wants to claim weights "do not matter", it should also report per-objective outcomes or a hypervolume/ε-indicator across EA candidates. Otherwise reviewers may argue that RMS only collapses the differences.
- Following Hayes Sec. 8.3, final candidates should be re-evaluated with fresh seeds and episodes (separate from EA fitness) to separate real weight effects from selection noise.

### Gaps
- No MORL-specific source was found that discusses IGD in depth. IGD/IGD+ references would come from EMO (e.g., Coello & Reyes Sierra; Ishibuchi et al. 2015 for IGD+), which were not verified in this session.
