# Statistical rigor in RL evaluation, compute-matched comparison, noisy expensive optimization, and publishability of rigorous negative results

Scope note: these notes support (a) a pre-registered, multi-seed, compute-matched evaluation protocol and (b) a null/negative finding: evolutionary reward-weight search for PPO is not worth its compute compared with training PPO longer. Every bibliographic item below was checked against a primary or index page (publisher, PMLR, JMLR, arXiv, DBLP, institutional repository) during this session, unless it is marked UNVERIFIED. Where I only saw an abstract or a secondary summary, I say so.

## Q1. How many seeds and which statistical tests do the methodology papers recommend? What do they say about effect sizes versus seed variance?

### Takeaway
No methodology paper gives one universal seed count. All of them say the count must come from power or uncertainty analysis, that 5 or fewer seeds is usually too few, and that differences of a size typical in RL papers can come from seeds alone. The current consensus is to report interval estimates (stratified bootstrap CIs, IQM, performance profiles, probability of improvement) rather than point estimates or bare p-values, to keep tuning seeds separate from test seeds, and to run a power analysis (target power 0.8) when choosing N.

### Cited Findings
**Henderson, Islam, Bachman, Pineau, Precup & Meger (2018), "Deep Reinforcement Learning That Matters", AAAI-18 (Proc. AAAI 32(1)); arXiv:1709.06560**
- They ran ten TRPO trials on HalfCheetah-v1 with identical hyperparameters, differing only in seed, and split them into two groups of five. The two group means formed statistically different distributions (2-sample t-test t = -9.0916, p = 0.0016). The paper says "the variance between runs is enough to create statistically different distributions" — [ar5iv full text](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1709.06560); [AAAI OJS](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/11694)
- In the supplement, the same result held for DDPG and TRPO on two environments: "statistically different distributions can be attained just from the random seeds with the same exact hyperparameters" — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1709.06560)
- The paper declines to set a number: "there can be no specific number of trials specified as a recommendation". It recommends power analysis, running more trials when CIs are wide, and warns against reporting top-N trials or averaging fewer than five — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1709.06560)
- Tests it uses: 2-sample t-test, Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, and bootstrap 95% CIs (10,000 resamples). Worked example, Walker2d ACKTR vs DDPG: t = 1.03, p = 0.334; KS = 0.40, p = 0.697; bootstrapped percent difference 44.47% with CI (-80.62%, 111.72%). A 44% apparent gap was therefore not significant — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1709.06560)
- Reward scaling had large, environment-inconsistent effects on DDPG. Hyperparameters "can have significantly different effects across algorithms and environments." The central recommendation is to report all hyperparameters, implementation details, setup and evaluation method — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1709.06560)

**Colas, Sigaud & Oudeyer (2018), "How Many Random Seeds? Statistical Power Analysis in Deep Reinforcement Learning Experiments", arXiv:1806.08295** (a preprint/tutorial; I did not confirm a peer-reviewed venue)
- A tutorial on choosing N seeds from Type-I and Type-II error rates, using Welch's t-test and a bootstrap CI test. It also discusses how violated assumptions (non-normality, unequal variance) distort error rates — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.08295v2); [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1806.08295)
- N is chosen to reach a theoretical power of 1−β = 0.8. In the worked example, a difference that was not significant at N=5 became significant at N=10 (Welch p = 0.0037) — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1806.08295)
- Motivation: all deep RL papers reviewed by Henderson et al. used 5 seeds or fewer — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1806.08295)
- Code (welch_test, bootstrap_test, compute_beta, a false-negative-rate vs sample-size plot) — [GitHub flowersteam/rl-difference-testing](https://github.com/flowersteam/rl-difference-testing)
- Follow-up by the same group: Colas, Sigaud & Oudeyer (2019), "A Hitchhiker's Guide to Statistical Comparisons of Reinforcement Learning Algorithms", arXiv:1904.06979. It studies power as a function of seeds and effect size across several tests — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.06979v1.pdf) (I saw only the abstract-level description; its exact venue is not confirmed here)

**Agarwal, Schwarzer, Castro, Courville & Bellemare (2021), "Deep Reinforcement Learning at the Edge of the Statistical Precipice", NeurIPS 2021 (Outstanding Paper Award); arXiv:2108.13264; library `rliable`**
- Deep RL papers usually use 3-10 runs per task. Five runs on 50+ Atari games under the standard protocol costs more than 1,000 GPU-days — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2108.13264); [NeurIPS PDF](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2021/file/f514cec81cb148559cf475e7426eed5e-Paper.pdf)
- Atari 100k case study: 100 runs per algorithm on 26 games, subsampled to 3-100 runs. Sample medians vary a lot and are biased. For SPR, the bias between 5 and 100 runs (+0.03) is about 36% of its claimed mean improvement over DrQ(ε) (+0.08). Median CIs became reasonably tight only at about 50-100 runs. With 100 runs, a comparison with no true difference could still show deviations of up to 20% — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2108.13264)
- Recommendations: stratified bootstrap CIs (percentile intervals work for as few as 10 runs); performance profiles (score distributions); interquartile mean (IQM), which is more statistically efficient than the median and gives much smaller CIs; probability of improvement; optimality gap. Prefer CIs over p-value thresholds — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2108.13264); [Google Research blog](https://research.google/blog/rliable-towards-reliable-evaluation-reporting-in-reinforcement-learning/)
- Re-analysis of published claims: on DeepMind Control, most algorithms did not consistently outrank the methods they claimed to beat. On Procgen (10 runs/task), UCB-DrAC had only a 40-50% probability of improving on PLR, and several reported improvements were only 50-70% likely — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2108.13264)

**Patterson, Neumann, White & White (2024), "Empirical Design in Reinforcement Learning", JMLR 25(318):1-63; arXiv:2304.01315**
- A 63-page guide covering the statistical assumptions behind common performance measures, how to characterize performance variation and stability, hypothesis testing, comparing multiple agents, building baselines, hyperparameters and experimenter bias. It argues that the scale of current experiments often "conflict[s] with the need for statistical evidence, especially when comparing algorithms" — [JMLR](https://jmlr.org/papers/v25/23-0183.html); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.01315)
- (I saw the abstract-level content only. Specific seed-count numbers in the paper are not verified here.)

**Jordan, Chandak, Cohen, Zhang & Thomas (2020), "Evaluating the Performance of Reinforcement Learning Algorithms", ICML 2020, PMLR 119:4962-4973; arXiv:2006.16958**
- Argues that inconsistent RL results come from flawed evaluation metrics. It proposes evaluating the complete algorithm, including how its hyperparameters are set (manual, heuristic or adaptive), rather than its peak performance after tuning. It introduces performance percentiles for cross-environment normalization and a game-theoretic aggregate, and reports CIs on the aggregate — [PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v119/jordan20a.html); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.16958); [ICML slides](https://ICML.cc/media/icml-2020/Slides/6301.pdf)

**Eimer, Lindauer & Raileanu (2023), "Hyperparameters in Reinforcement Learning and How To Tune Them", ICML 2023, PMLR 202:9104-9149; arXiv:2306.01324**
- The hyperparameter landscape "can strongly depend on the tuning seed which may lead to overfitting." They recommend separating tuning and test seeds — [PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v202/eimer23a.html)
- Seed variability "is likely a bigger difficulty factor for HPO in RL" than the landscape itself, which is "fairly smooth" (Sec. 4.2). Seed variance is largest for medium-performing configurations (Sec. 4.2) — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2306.01324)
- Recommends at least 5 tuning seeds (Appendix A). Reporting tuned seeds for the proposed method and test seeds for baselines "is an unfair comparison which can lead to wrong conclusions" (Sec. 4.3). Report tuning seeds, test seeds and the exact selection protocol, and use the same budget and search space for every method (Sec. 6) — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2306.01324)
- The project page warns that "even an overlap in seeds can completely invalidate the results" — [automl.org](https://www.automl.org/hyperparameter-tuning)

### Inferences
- For a single-environment PPO study, a defensible protocol has these parts: pre-registered N chosen by a Colas-style power analysis at a stated minimum effect of interest; at least 10 independent seeds per arm (Agarwal's percentile bootstrap works from about 10 runs); IQM or mean with stratified/percentile bootstrap 95% CIs plus probability of improvement; Welch's t-test only as a secondary measure; and disjoint tuning and test seeds (Eimer).
- Henderson's two-groups-of-five result is the canonical citation for "an effect of the size we observe can come from seeds alone." Agarwal's Procgen and DMC re-analyses are the canonical citation for "many published RL improvements disappear under interval estimation."

### Gaps
- I did not extract Colas et al.'s exact power tables (seeds needed for a given standardized effect size). Pull them from the PDF if a specific number is needed.
- Patterson et al.'s specific recommendations (e.g., the number of runs and their treatment of hyperparameter sensitivity curves) were not verified beyond the abstract.

## Q2. What evidence shows that hyperparameter or reward tuning gains disappear under compute-matched comparison or are dominated by seed noise?

### Takeaway
Across RL, GANs, language models, recommender systems and NAS, large controlled studies find that claimed algorithmic gains shrink or vanish when baselines get equal tuning or compute, when implementation details are controlled, or when seed variance is measured properly. Random search, and random search given twice the budget, are standard strong compute baselines. I found no paper that tests "train PPO longer vs evolutionary reward-weight search" directly. That gap is what our study fills.

### Cited Findings
- **Engstrom, Ilyas, Santurkar, Tsipras, Janoos, Rudolph & Madry (2020), "Implementation Matters in Deep Policy Gradients: A Case Study on PPO and TRPO", ICLR 2020; arXiv:2005.12729.** Code-level optimizations (including reward scaling/normalization) account for most of PPO's gain in cumulative reward over TRPO, matter more than the choice of PPO vs TRPO itself, and fundamentally change how trust regions are enforced. Attributing gains in deep RL is hard without controlling these details — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12729); [ICLR poster](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2020/poster/1752). (Only "Engstrom, Ilyas et al." was verified in search results; the remaining co-authors are from memory — confirm against the PDF.)
- **Andrychowicz, Raichuk, Stańczyk, Orsini, Girgin, Marinier, Hussenot, Geist, Pietquin, Michalski, Gelly & Bachem (2021), "What Matters for On-Policy Deep Actor-Critic Methods? A Large-Scale Study", ICLR 2021 (arXiv title: "What Matters In On-Policy Reinforcement Learning? A Large-Scale Empirical Study", arXiv:2006.05990).** They implemented more than 50 design choices in one framework and trained over 250,000 agents on 5 continuous-control environments. Low- and high-level design decisions "strongly affect the performance", are rarely reported, and make it hard to tell where progress comes from — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.05990); [Google Research](https://research.google/pubs/what-matters-for-on-policy-deep-actor-critic-methods-a-large-scale-study/). (The full 12-author list is from memory of the paper header. Confirm it against the PDF before typesetting.)
- **Henderson et al. (2018), AAAI** (see Q1). Seed-only differences reach p = 0.0016. Reward scale changes DDPG results inconsistently across environments — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1709.06560)
- **Eimer et al. (2023), ICML.** Incumbents from HPO often do much worse on held-out seeds ("The performance difference between tuning and testing is significant in many cases", Sec. 4.3). PB2 (a population-based method) showed large test-time drops, sometimes around 5x on Bigfish. Population-based methods tended to produce "fairly static configurations instead of flexible schedules" (Sec. 7). Cheap HPO with 16-64 full runs could match or beat a hand-tuned IDAAC baseline that used about 810 runs (Sec. 5) — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2306.01324)
- **Bergstra & Bengio (2012), "Random Search for Hyper-Parameter Optimization", JMLR 13:281-305.** With the same budget, random search matched or beat manual-plus-grid search on deep belief networks: statistically equal on 4 of 7 datasets, better on 1 of 7. The explanation is low effective dimensionality: only a few hyperparameters matter, and which ones differ by dataset — [JMLR](https://jmlr.org/papers/v13/bergstra12a.html)
- **Li, Jamieson, DeSalvo, Rostamizadeh & Talwalkar (2018), "Hyperband: A Novel Bandit-Based Approach to Hyperparameter Optimization", JMLR 18(185):1-52.** It compares against a "random 2x" baseline (random search with twice the budget). The authors' project page says running random search twice as long beat SMAC and TPE, so a doubled-budget naive baseline is an established compute-matched control — [JMLR](https://jmlr.org/papers/v18/16-558.html); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1603.06560); [project page](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~jamieson/hyperband.html) (secondary for the 2x claim; check the paper's figures for exact numbers)
- **Li & Talwalkar (2020), "Random Search and Reproducibility for Neural Architecture Search", UAI 2019, PMLR 115.** Random search with early stopping performs at least as well as ENAS on PTB and CIFAR-10. None of the surveyed NAS methods was exactly reproducible, partly because random seeds were not reported — [PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v115/li20c.html); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.07638)
- **Lucic, Kurach, Michalski, Gelly & Bousquet (2018), "Are GANs Created Equal? A Large-Scale Study", NeurIPS 2018.** With enough compute for hyperparameter search and random restarts, nearly all GAN variants reach similar FID, with no consistent winner over the original non-saturating GAN. They recommend reporting the distribution of results, not the best run. The study cost about 6.85 P100 GPU-years — [NeurIPS](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/2018/hash/e46de7e1bcaaced9a54f1e9d0d2f800d-Abstract.html); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.10337)
- **Melis, Dyer & Blunsom (2018), "On the State of the Art of Evaluation in Neural Language Models", ICLR 2018.** Under large-scale automatic black-box hyperparameter tuning, well-regularized standard LSTMs beat newer architectures. Prior comparisons used "differing codebases and limited computational resources" — [ICLR](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2018/poster/214); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.05589v2)
- **Ferrari Dacrema, Boglio, Cremonesi & Jannach (2021), "A Troubling Analysis of Reproducibility and Progress in Recommender Systems Research", ACM TOIS 39(2), Art. 20, 49 pp.** 11 of 12 reproducible neural top-n recommenders could be beaten by conceptually simple, properly tuned methods. The conference precursor (RecSys 2019, "Are We Really Making Much Progress?") won Best Long Paper — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.07698); [PoliMi PDF](https://re.public.polimi.it/retrieve/e68a57ea-dc32-45c3-a815-ca28d37aa6c6/a-troubling-analysis-of-reproducibility-and-progress-in-recommender-systems-research.pdf)

### Inferences
- The shared lesson is that once tuning, compute and seed variance are equalized, many apparent gains disappear. Our "train longer" arm plays the same role as "random 2x" in Hyperband and as the properly tuned simple baseline in Melis, Lucic and Dacrema: a naive use of the same compute.
- Engstrom's finding that reward scaling and normalization are code-level details with large effects supports treating reward-weight changes as a hyperparameter-like intervention. Such interventions must be compared against equal-compute alternatives and judged against seed variance.
- Eimer's PB2 generalization drops and the "static configurations" observation fit a plausible mechanism: evolutionary or population search over reward weights overfits to tuning seeds.

### Gaps
- I found no published study that compares evolutionary reward-weight (multi-objective scalarization) search for PPO against simply training PPO longer at equal environment steps or wall-clock. This is the gap our paper addresses, but I could not cite a precedent.
- I did not verify Dehghani et al. "The Efficiency Misnomer" (ICLR 2022), which argues for reporting several cost indicators. Treat it as UNVERIFIED until checked.
- Related AutoRL work on reward shaping by evolution (e.g., Faust, Francis & Mehta, "Evolving Rewards to Automate Reinforcement Learning", 2019) was not verified this session. It is UNVERIFIED.

## Q3. What are the established remedies for the winner's curse in noisy evolutionary optimization (resampling, re-evaluation of elites, statistical selection)?

### Takeaway
Picking the best of many noisy estimates overstates the true value of the pick, even when every estimate is unbiased (the optimizer's curse). The EC literature has five standard countermeasures: explicit resampling (fixed or adaptive, sequential), implicit averaging via larger populations, fitness estimation or models, modified search strategies, and statistically aware selection (threshold or ranking-and-selection / OCBA). Outside EC, the remedy is Bayesian shrinkage, or confirmation on fresh independent evaluations. Our held-out re-evaluation of the selected weights on new seeds is exactly that remedy.

### Cited Findings
- **Smith & Winkler (2006), "The Optimizer's Curse: Skepticism and Postdecision Surprise in Decision Analysis", Management Science 52(3):311-322, DOI 10.1287/mnsc.1050.0451.** When you choose the alternative with the highest estimated value, its realized value tends to fall short of the estimate even if each estimate is unbiased, so the selection itself creates the bias. The remedy is Bayesian adjustment of estimates ("disciplined skepticism") — [RePEc/IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v52y2006i3p311-322.html); [Duke record](https://fds.duke.edu/db/aas/stat/faculty/bob.winkler/publications/278471)
- **Correction (2007):** the authors' correction note says the "expected disappointment" values in Fig. 3 were wrong because of a simulation error. The true disappointment is "substantially larger and decreases more slowly" than the paper showed. Cite the correction too if using those figures — [correction PDF](https://jimsmith.host.dartmouth.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/pds_correction.pdf)
- **Jin & Branke (2005), "Evolutionary Optimization in Uncertain Environments—A Survey", IEEE TEVC 9(3):303-317, DOI 10.1109/TEVC.2005.846356.** The canonical survey. It covers noisy fitness, robustness, fitness approximation and dynamic environments, and gives methods for noisy fitness functions — [Bielefeld record](https://pub.uni-bielefeld.de/record/2978653); [BibBase](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/jin-branke-evolutionaryoptimizationinuncertainenvironmentsasurvey-2005). (The full-text PDF link returned 404 this session, so I did not quote specific claims such as the sqrt(n) reduction in noise standard deviation from resampling.)
- **Rakshit, Konar & Das (2017), "Noisy Evolutionary Optimization Algorithms – A Comprehensive Survey", Swarm and Evolutionary Computation 33:18-45, DOI 10.1016/j.swevo.2016.09.002 (online 2016).** Covers single- and multi-objective noisy EAs. When noise statistics are unknown, selection struggles to tell good trial solutions from bad ones. The abstract lists five remedy families: "(i) fitness sampling of individual trial solution", "(ii) fitness estimation of noisy samples", "(iii) dynamic population sizing over the generations", "(iv) adaptation of the evolutionary search strategy", "(v) modification in the selection strategy" — [ISI repository](https://digitalcommons.isical.ac.in/journal-articles/2624). An expanded version appears as a chapter in Rakshit, Konar & Das (2018), *Principles in Noisy Optimization*, Springer — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-981-10-8642-7_6)
- **Branke & Schmidt (2003), "Selection in the Presence of Noise", GECCO 2003.** States the trade-off between spending evaluations on reducing noise and spending them on evaluating more solutions — [GECCO 2003 bib](https://cs.york.ac.uk/rts/docs/GECCO_2003/bibs/2723/27230766.htm)
- **Branke & Schmidt (2004), "Sequential Sampling in Noisy Environments", PPSN VIII.** Allocates samples sequentially and adaptively, only until the selection decision is statistically sound — [UT Austin reading list entry](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~shivaram/readings/b2hd-BrankeSchmidt2004.html)
- **Ranking and selection / OCBA:** Branke, Chick & Schmidt (2007, Management Science) compare the three main approaches to selecting the best under noise: indifference-zone, value of information, and OCBA. OCBA-m (selecting the top-m designs, as an EA does when picking elites) was proposed by Chen, He & Fu (2008) and improved population-based search — [INSEAD PDF of Branke, Chick & Schmidt](https://www.insead.edu/sites/insead/files/assets/faculty-personal-site/stephen-e-chick/documents/new%20development%20in%20ranking%20and%20selection-an%20empirical%20comparison%20of%20the%20three%20main%20approaches.pdf); [WSC 2012 paper discussing OCBA-m](https://www.informs-sim.org/wsc12papers/includes/files/con371.pdf). (The venue and year for Chen, He & Fu came through search snippets. Verify before citing.)
- **Schmidt (2007), "Evolutionary Computation in Stochastic Environments", PhD dissertation, Univ. Karlsruhe.** Covers OCBA, sequential sampling and noise-adjusted selection in EAs — [KIT repository](https://publikationen.bibliothek.kit.edu/1000006634/2637)

### Inferences
- In our setting, each candidate reward-weight vector's fitness is a noisy PPO training outcome, and the EA keeps the apparent best. That is a textbook optimizer's curse: the elite's in-search fitness is biased upward. The standard remedies are re-evaluating the final elite(s) on fresh, independent seeds (out-of-sample confirmation), resampling or sequential sampling of elites, R&S/OCBA-style selection, or Bayesian shrinkage (Smith & Winkler).
- Each remedy costs extra evaluations. This strengthens the null result: an EA with adequate noise handling costs even more compute, so the cost-benefit comparison with training longer becomes even less favorable.
- Our pre-registered held-out re-evaluation is a direct application of the remedy that Smith & Winkler and the EC noise surveys prescribe. It also explains why the in-search gains shrink on the test seeds.

### Gaps
- I did not get quotations from the Jin & Branke full text (PDF 404) or the Rakshit full text (the PDF could not be parsed here). For details such as explicit vs implicit averaging and the sqrt(n) noise reduction, read the papers directly.
- I did not verify the Arnold & Beyer analyses of evolution strategies under noise this session.
- I did not verify RL-specific "winner's curse in HPO" papers. Eimer et al.'s tune/test seed gap is the closest verified evidence.

## Q4. Are there rigorous negative-result or benchmark papers accepted in Q1 journals, and what infrastructure exists for pre-registration and negative results?

### Takeaway
Yes. Rigorous "the claimed gains don't hold up" papers appear in top journals and venues: ACM TOIS (Dacrema et al.), Nature Machine Intelligence (Kudela), JMLR (Patterson et al.; Bergstra & Bengio), IEEE TEVC (benchmarking methodology papers and a special issue), and as award-winning NeurIPS and ICLR papers (Agarwal; Lucic; Melis). Pre-registration has a concrete ML precedent (NeurIPS 2020/2021 pre-registration workshops, PMLR 148). Empirical Software Engineering runs a formal Registered Reports track. The ICBINB workshop series explicitly solicits negative results. I found no direct evidence about how IoT/edge Q1 reviewers (FGCS, IEEE IoT-J, TPDS) view negative results.

### Cited Findings
**Negative or benchmark results in strong venues**
- **ACM TOIS (Q1):** Ferrari Dacrema et al. (2021), 39(2) Art. 20. 11 of 12 neural recommenders were beaten by simple tuned baselines, and the paper criticizes researcher freedom in choosing metrics, protocols, datasets and baselines — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.07698)
- **Nature Machine Intelligence:** Kudela (2022), "A critical problem in benchmarking and analysis of evolutionary computation methods", 4:1238-1245, DOI 10.1038/s42256-022-00579-0. Seven recent EC methods contain a center-bias operator that makes their benchmark comparisons "meaningless". A 2023 arXiv follow-up ("The Evolutionary Computation Methods No One Should Use", arXiv:2301.01984) found the problem in 47 of 90 methods — [Nature MI](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42256-022-00579-0); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.01984v1)
- **IEEE TEVC methodology papers** (found via a secondary search; verify page numbers before citing): Liu et al. (2020), "Paradoxes in Numerical Comparison of Optimization Algorithms", IEEE TEVC 24(4), shows that comparison methods can produce contradictory rankings. Bäck, Doerr, Sendhoff, Stützle et al. (These are only partly verified: I identified them from snippets, not publisher pages.)

**Pre-registration and negative-results venues**
- **NeurIPS 2020 workshop**, "The pre-registration experiment: an alternative publication model for machine learning research" (Bertinetto, Henriques, Albanie, Paganini, Varol; 11 Dec 2020; proceedings PMLR vol. 148). Papers are reviewed without results, on the protocol alone. Reviewers judge the experimental design "rather than comparing numeric results." The organizers argue against "heavy reliance upon performance as a proxy for scientific progress." It follows a small computer-vision trial (Henriques et al., 2019) — [NeurIPS 2020 workshop page](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2020/workshop/16158); [DBLP](https://dblp.uni-trier.de/rec/conf/preregister/2020.html)
- **NeurIPS 2021 follow-up**, "The pre-registration workshop" — [NeurIPS 2021](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2021/workshop/21885)
- **ICBINB ("I Can't Believe It's Not Better") workshops:** NeurIPS 2020 (probabilistic ML, "ideas that failed in practice"), NeurIPS 2022 ("Understanding Deep Learning Through Empirical Falsification"), NeurIPS 2023 ("Failure Modes in the Age of Foundation Models", with an "Entropic Award" for the most surprising negative result), ICLR 2025. The series promotes "slow science" against "leaderboard-ism" — [NeurIPS 2020](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2020/workshop/16124); [NeurIPS 2022](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2022/workshop/49960); [ICBINB 2023 CfP](https://sites.google.com/view/icbinb-2023/submit); [ICLR 2025](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2025/workshop/23987)
- **Empirical Software Engineering (Springer, Q1) Registered Reports**, offered since 2019. Stage 1 is protocol review at a partner conference (MSR, ESEM, ICSME), leading to in-principle acceptance. Stage 2 is the full paper in EMSE, where reviewers check fidelity to the protocol and whether deviations are justified. The stated aim is to prevent HARKing — [EMSE RR page](https://emsejournal.github.io/registered_reports); [ESEM 2024 RR track](https://conf.researchr.org/track/esem-2024/esem-2024-registered-reports); [Ernst & Baldassarre editorial, EMSE 2023](https://rd.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007%2Fs10664-022-10277-5.pdf)

### Inferences
- A Q1 IoT/edge submission can present its null result as a methodology and benchmark contribution: pre-registered protocol, compute-matched baseline, held-out seeds, interval estimates.
